MONTRONEDSP / LINUX

Linux for Musicians

A home for our Linux instruments, open-source work and practical notes on making music with an open platform.

01 / THE PLATFORM

A system you can make your own.

We are strong supporters of Linux and open-source software. For us, music tools should be understandable, adaptable and open to experimentation. Our Linux work brings those values into the instruments we build.

More music from hardware you already own.

A carefully chosen Linux setup can make economical use of memory and processing power. A lighter desktop and fewer background services can leave more of your computer’s resources available for music, and make an older laptop or desktop useful again.

Before replacing a computer you already own, consider giving it a new role: a recording station, a dedicated software instrument or a place to develop ideas. Choose a currently supported system that suits its specifications, then test your DAW, plugins and audio interface with a realistic session. The gains depend on the hardware and workload; a lighter system can reduce overhead, but it cannot remove the limits of the processor or memory.

02 / YOUR OPERATING SYSTEM

Start where you feel at home.

The distribution matters less than feeling comfortable with your system. Choose the one that feels familiar to you, then check that it supports your audio interface, DAW and plugins. Our straightforward starting recommendation is Ubuntu.

OUR PRIMARY RECOMMENDATION

For most musicians moving to Linux, we recommend Ubuntu LTS first. It offers an approachable desktop, a well-documented setup and a long-term support cycle: a practical foundation for a studio you want to get to know and keep using.

Explore Ubuntu Desktop

OUR PERSONAL CHOICE

For producers coming from a traditional desktop setup, our personal choice is SteamOS on compatible hardware. Based on Arch Linux, it pairs Valve’s integrated experience with a full KDE Plasma desktop in Desktop Mode. Its appeal for us is the everyday experience: familiar windows, a taskbar and room to work across applications.

Why we like it

An easy-to-use experience on supported devices, with a flexible desktop for multitasking. Connect a screen, keyboard and mouse to move between your DAW, files and reference material in Desktop Mode. Plasma lets you adapt the workspace to your habits.

What needs planning

The operating-system image is read-only by default. Installing system packages is less straightforward, and changes outside Flatpak can disappear after an OS update. SteamOS is built around gaming hardware; confirm device support and test your audio interface, MIDI and low-latency workflow before relying on it for sessions.

Work with the system

Use Discover and Flatpak for available desktop apps. Keep projects and supported portable tools in your home folder, and use your DAW’s user plugin locations. Sandboxed hosts may need explicit access to plugins, files and devices; check the host’s installation guidance. Some studio tools still require a conventional distribution.

Valve documents how to disable read-only protection for advanced changes, but system-package changes may be lost on update. We would not make that the starting point for a dependable studio. Ubuntu with KDE Plasma is another route if you want a familiar desktop and conventional package management.

Check Valve’s current hardware guidance before installing. Support outside officially supported devices is limited, and the full-install option wipes the target device: back up your work first.

03 / YOUR DAW

Choose the way you work.

Our primary commercial recommendations for Linux are REAPER and Bitwig Studio. For an open-source studio you can shape around your own needs, we recommend Ardour.

COMMERCIAL / LINUX

REAPER

REAPER is our first recommendation for a flexible recording, editing and mixing workflow. Its routing, custom actions and ReaScript support give you room to build a working environment around your habits.

Explore REAPER

COMMERCIAL / LINUX

Bitwig Studio

Bitwig Studio is our other primary commercial choice, especially for electronic music, clip-based ideas and sound design. Its modulation system and modular approach make it a natural place to explore movement and performance.

Explore Bitwig Studio

OPEN SOURCE / LINUX

Ardour

Ardour is our recommended open-source route for recording, editing and mixing on Linux. Its appeal goes beyond using an open application: you can develop a workflow that reflects how you make music.

Build your own starting point.

Create session templates with your tracks, buses, plugins and connections already in place. Start each project with a studio layout that makes sense to you.

Make repeated tasks your own.

Use Lua scripting to add custom actions, batch operations and automation workflows. Ardour includes a script editor for adapting scripts inside the application.

Take the workflow further.

With the source available, developers can explore deeper changes, build a tailored version and contribute improvements back to the project. That scope for adaptation is why we see Ardour as a particularly valuable route for an open Linux studio.
